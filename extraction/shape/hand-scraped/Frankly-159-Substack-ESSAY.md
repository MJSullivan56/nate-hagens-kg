What is the "real work" of our time?

Recently I’ve been asking myself a question more and more: “What are we really trying to do with this work?” I suppose embedded in that is another question: “Why do I do this work?” This week’s essay addresses that question. It may be a bit intense and raw, but I also hope it is clarifying and helpful.

As I’ve reflected on this, my reason for this work is that I deeply care about our biophysical and ecological reality, and that our cultural expectations and institutional plans for the future are misaligned from that reality. There’s a large, and actually widening, gap between the two. I truly feel like sharing the things I know can, in some small way, help steer the future away from the current default. I want to educate, inform, inspire, and shine a light on the real paths ahead of us, instead of the cargo-cult, high-status, comforting, and grandiose narratives that are so popular in the news and on social media.

So that’s why I do this work. But what is “the real work?” There are lots of things we would like to improve about the future. I’ve said before that what we face is not a problem with a solution, but a predicament with responses. A problem is something you solve and move on from, while a predicament is something you live through – and how we live through this one will matter enormously.

To me, the question about the work ahead of us is really a question about responses. Given the biophysical and ecological systems science that informs the logic of The Great Simplification, I think it boils down to four core categories of responses which must be pursued by this platform and by millions of people around the world, many of whom will never meet one another.

The Financial Cul-De-Sac

To briefly summarize the core logic of our predicament: We are a social species that spread out across the world once hierarchy and surplus became prominent, and then snowballed. We started to mine historic caches of oil, gas, coal, and nonrenewable minerals at high quality and scale. Once combined with machines, these turbocharged our global economies, our expectations, and our civilizational complexity.

The financial claims we make on the resources we think we have will eventually run smack into the biophysical reality if the Earth.

Now we’re reaching a phase shift in that story. There is an economic cul-de-sac ahead of us where the financial claims on our reality start to meaningfully exceed our biophysical and ecological reality. I believe some sort of deflationary depression is now inevitable. The financial claims we make on the resources we believe we have will eventually run smack against the reality of what we actually have, Wile E. Coyote-style. This will likely manifest first in the global bond markets, then in the currency markets. As things get scarcer, and as raw material, commodity, and energy prices go up, our inability to keep real productivity and real GDP at pace with expectations will cause the financial system to cascade downward into a 1929-style deflationary depression.

Bend, Don’t Break

So let’s get to the first of four categories of “the real work”: helping society to bend and not break.

“Bending” is a strained continuation of interconnectivity and social contracts, while “breaking” is a form of civilizational collapse. When I reference the Great Simplification, I’m not necessarily describing systemic collapse, but rather the overlay of our financial architectures taking a significant haircut and the resulting complexity and supply chain risks that follow.

The blue data line displays moderate fluctuations with a notable sharp increase and peak occurring around 2021-2022. Two shaded grey vertical regions indicate periods of U.S. economic recession, specifically around 2008-2009 and early 2020.


We narrowly avoided a deflationary depression in 2008 and 2020, shown in the shaded section on this graph.

This almost happened in 2008 and 2020. We used some of our remaining bullets by having central banks take over part of the commercial banks’ money creation, “too big to fail” guarantees, and artificial interest rates: the financial papering-over of our problems. But the trust in those fixes, and the number of times we can use them, is declining.

Critically, breaking at this point doesn’t just drop our profits ten or twenty percent and then slingshot us back up to new heights. It means we drop down a ladder we might not be able to climb up again, because we’ve kicked out some of the rungs on our way up.

So how do we maintain connectivity, even with lower material throughput? Said differently, how do we navigate partial failure of this integrated, mutually contingent set of advantages – including ten-thousand-mile supply chains, instantaneous letters of credit, cheap global energy, global peace, functioning currencies, and immediate transactions – without breaking?

A diverse group of nine men and women of various ages sitting in a circle on chairs, engaging in a group discussion in a spacious, brightly lit community room with hardwood floors.

Build social capital where you live across social groups and identities.
In our current system, we are completely dependent on undersea internet cables, surgical gloves and fertilizer made in another country, over 30,000 unique products in every grocery store, and all the other things we assume will be here tomorrow because of intricate, complicated supply chains that are most vulnerable to a rupture in cheap energy and credit. So the work to “bend” means to re-localize supply chains, especially for basic goods: ensuring access to food, medicine, and materials that don’t depend on a six-continent journey. The work is also expanding your social capital where you live, meeting people near you across social groups and identities – Republican, Democrat, left, right, rich, poor, old, and young. Those bonds will benefit us in almost all future scenarios.

Bending can mean more communities and bioregions engaging in scenario planning ahead of a crisis, which builds trust and invites imagination and creativity before they’re needed. It might also mean finding Goldilocks technology, which refers to tools that get us eighty percent of the benefit with twenty percent of the resources and can be maintained and repaired locally without a global just-in-time umbilical cord. These might include things like sodium batteries, which are less efficient than lithium, but cheaper and based on far more abundant raw materials. Advanced policy plays a role in bending as well: working on interventions that might seem too radical today, but may prove to be necessary in the not-too-distant future. We’ve talked a lot about these interventions before, and I’ll increasingly explore these various fronts through interviews with experts and deeper solo dives.

Of course, few people who believe the future will be shinier than the present are going to work on these things, which is why sharing this story with others is so important. Because people who can see what’s coming can better respond with agency and creativity instead of panic.

No Nuclear War

The second category is not one that I’m working on publicly, but it is a core part of the real work. It’s avoiding World War III, but more specifically avoiding a strategic nuclear exchange among great powers in the next few decades.

The map highlights nine nuclear-armed states, using callouts to display the estimated number of warheads for each country and arrows indicating whether stockpiles are increasing or decreasing. A text box explains that the world's combined inventory is approximately 12,187 warheads, with Russia and the United States holding about 90% of that total.

Nine countries currently hold over 12,000 nuclear warheads.
Humanity doesn’t have the best track record when it comes to difficult times of energy descent. A mishandled Great Simplification is precisely the environment in which nations become desperate, so it’s clear to me that energy descent and conflict risk are deeply coupled. This is why geopolitics keeps showing up on what is nominally an ecology podcast. There are nine countries that have over 12,000 nuclear warheads, and most of them are building more all the time. Whether it’s via spite or aggression or mistake, it’s imperative that we avoid any exchange of nuclear weapons, because none of the other work matters if that ends up being our path.

Behind the scenes, I do work on mitigating these risks, even though I don’t talk a lot about it. In my opinion there should be a huge disarmament campaign happening right now, but there are crickets as far as I can tell. People should be pouring their lives into this, doing anything to accomplish it, and all I really see are yawns and talk. Personally, I think we are no more than a few years from the next nuclear weapon being detonated against other humans.

In the past, people (including superpowers) feared nuclear weapons in a way we just don’t today. There were large and successful grassroots, nongovernmental, and governmental disarmament campaigns resulting in drawdown treaties and incentives to prevent the “nuclear club” from growing. But it’s now been over eighty years since a nuclear blast was used against human beings, and the idea of nuclear holocaust has become a sort of dystopian movie trope, like a zombie apocalypse. People who conducted those past disarmament campaigns were scared to their bones, including leaders. Today, people are mostly scared of the stock market going down.

The delicate logic of war avoidance is breaking down as nations stop being so careful. There are new leaders who may not even realize that shrinking the “launch it or lose it” time from twenty minutes to three minutes is not a good idea. Most people don’t know that there are thresholds of nuclear exchange that would trigger nuclear winters and starve nearly everyone on the planet within a few years, including other species. There are actuarial charts that show you how long you might be expected to live in these scenarios.

The most likely scenario is probably not a madman firing off a nuke. The most likely scenario is a “sane” political leader, being advised by “sane” technocrats who have made the calculation (or increasingly likely, let AI make the calculation for them) that the tactical and strategic benefits of using a nuclear weapon outweigh the risks. If you look at the most catastrophically “bad” historical decisions, they are often the result of a series of “good” decisions taken in isolation for tactical reasons. But sometimes, put together, those logical-at-the-time decisions lead to catastrophe, because the consequences were either unanticipated or discounted. I think it’s dangerous to think that only crazy people will use nukes. I’ve learned that “sane” people are probably the most dangerous people of all.

I acknowledge it may seem odd to speak of fiscal depression in the same breath as nuclear annihilation, but they are not unrelated and neither is unlikely. I won’t give odds on nuclear annihilation, but I suspect it’s a lot higher than most expect. It is one of the main classes of possible outcomes in our own lifetime.

Even bringing this up on a public platform is really a downer, but if it isn’t addressed, how will things change? I don’t plan to focus on the nuclear threat too much publicly, but it is part of the work.

Midwife the Biosphere

The third main category of the real work is to midwife ecosystems, species, and the biosphere through the human-caused ecological bottlenecks of the 21st century, and to avoid the worst-case scenarios. It calls for us to breathe more life into the ecosphere, whether it’s improving the default global warming trajectory by a tenth of a degree, shifting ocean acidification by a tenth of a pH point, or causing ten percent more species to make it through to a future where humans have less impact. Any incremental support of the non-human living structures and non-human DNA on this planet falls into this category of work.

We often hear, when presented with climate and other ecological scenarios, that “the planet is going to be fine.” This is a hand-waving and dismissive response to real concerns, and I don’t personally believe this narrative, unless by “planet” people actually mean an inert bunch of elements. The planet will not be “fine” on our current trajectory if we regard “the planet” as biodiversity and complex life: the dolphins, gorillas, orangutans, humans, and all the other wonderful species we have today. Sure, life could revert to simplicity like slimes and jellies, and stay there until the planet as a whole is burned out by the sun. But I think we have to balance Gaia fantasies with Medea actualities, something a guest on the podcast, Peter Ward, has written books about. A planet of jellyfish and ants would mean the loss of incredible beings and diversity, and there is nothing “fine” about that.

This era of self-aware individual minds may be brief indeed. There is no logical reason to think it will happen again on Earth if we extinguish the existing lineages, as the “Earth clock” is already quite far along in time. The fossil record shows that humans don’t just get smarter and larger over time; they can also get smaller and evolve to chimp-sized brains, like they did on Flores. The only thing we truly know is that life as we experience and value it will not be fine, as it exists in a universe that is essentially hostile to such complex life. It seems robust and inevitable to us because that is the only kind of past that conscious abstracting minds like ours can ever see.

Midwifing the biosphere through local actions like ecosystem restoration.
So being in service of complex life is the third category of the work. Instead of extraction and convenience and comfort, we have to roll our sleeves up and contribute to defending and healing the broader planetary and ecological home, which most of our ancestors felt a kinship with. Regenerating living systems is a large category of work that I’ll increasingly highlight on this platform going forward.

Rekindle the Sacred

This all leads me to the fourth category, which underpins the three others. But this one is not an immediate intervention. It’s an ongoing process that will hopefully continue far beyond my lifetime. The fourth category of real work is to reimbue a sense of the sacred: that we are connected to the web of life.

A silhouette of a small bird perched on a berry-laden branch against a vibrant, glowing golden sky at dawn.

Sacred things - like seeing the birds in your field at dawn - must be seen as unacceptable to lose.

Every day, I wake up in the morning, have a coffee, and do some work that pays for my existence. What I hold sacred lies outside of this routine: the trees, the soil, the creatures, and the interconnected web of life that we’re a part of. I want to emphasize that “sacred” does not have to mean religious, although for many people it might take that shape. For these purposes, “sacred” refers to the felt sense that some things are not for sale, not substitutable, and not measurable in dollars. It’s the knowledge that some things are absolutely unacceptable to lose: a functioning ocean, a dawn chorus, the four-billion-year unbroken chain of living things that produced your dog and your children and you.

This sense was once a common feeling in our species. Many Indigenous cultures thought this way, and still do. But the Superorganism has kind of severed the relationship we have with our “interbeing,” as Charles Eisenstein would say (check out his recent TGS Episode). It’s like we were walking in a forest and came across a flashy casino and got distracted by the slot machines and all-you-can-eat smorgasbord. We went on a consumption bender, and then we were surprised and remorseful when we walked out of the casino and noticed that the forest had turned into a desert.

I think now is the time to support what’s left – to go out there and have a new story of humans and the biosphere. Perhaps what the old story could have been if random chance hadn’t given us flammable fossils. But we had to almost lose the biosphere due to the Carbon Pulse and eight billion of us consuming more than we need, so that we could have a spiritual recognition of this as a species. We had to change Earth’s ecology to appreciate its critical importance, and that it was always fragile enough for us to change it for the worse, forever.

Traditional cultures understood that humans do not mature automatically with age, so many of them used (and still use) initiations as a part of their tradition. These initiations often represent severance from the old identity, a rite of passage that allows one to return carrying new responsibilities. I believe humanity is in one of these rites of passage now. Gratitude, awe, and reciprocity with the living world, practiced imperfectly by individuals now, become the seeds of a culture we will not live to see. But we are integral to it happening at all. What we’re working toward are descendants who feel differently about life on Earth than our culture currently does. Perhaps that is generations away. But it has to start somewhere, and I think it has started. We need the good things on our planet to survive until that future, and that is the work we start now.

I’m older (and hopefully wiser) than I used to be. I’ve spent twenty years connecting all the dots that comprise the more-than-human predicament. Throughout all this, I’ve come to discover an answer to the deep question, “What is the meaning of our one life on this Earth?” What I’ve realized is that it’s not competition, accumulation, status, or things – it’s contributing to more life, especially complex life, and the conditions of Earth’s natural systems that support it. I think this was always true, but these systems were so historically stable that our “meaning” in this sense was expressed much more locally and tribally. Now the stakes are immensely higher, and the global is going to affect our local. There is more disruption of Earth’s stable seasons and natural rhythms coming no matter what. At the same time, more of us are feeling the truth of this psychological retethering to our past.

The economic supports that allowed such feelings to emerge are going to retreat, gradually then suddenly. I’ve suggested before that many climate campaigners will personally demand coal power for their own families when things get tough, when winters get colder and summers get hotter. The work to imbue and invite back in a sense of what is sacred stretches beyond our lifetimes. We have a sacred duty to protect and breathe life back into the Earth that first brought us life.

Corporations: The First Misaligned AI

There is obviously a great deal of work to do outside of these four categories, but these are the things our small organization is attempting to influence. Some of you might ask, especially due to the messages of some recent podcast guests, “Why don’t you include AI alignment risk as one of the must-haves of the work, given the short timelines and existential risks?” Well, like many other aspects of the metacrisis, there are capable people working full time on that. I hope and trust that they are successful. But I bring it up here for a reason.

When I was thinking about AI alignment, I had a striking thought relevant to today’s overview. We have already, for some time, had non-alignment between human good and human survival, driven by soulless, mindless, immortal automatons that are composed of multiple collaborating agents. We call them corporations! That non-alignment problem vastly predates speculations about AGI. We’re already living it, and have been for many years.

We’re already in a “tragedy of the commons” situation where the logic and motivator of corporations is, “If we don’t do it, someone else will. So we need to be first.“ Corporations, by the way, are legal persons in the United States. AI is not. The problem of hyperintelligent artificial beings that are ruthlessly non-aligned with human interests is hypothetical. The reality of artificial corporate beings that are ruthlessly and metabolically non-aligned with human interests is what we refer to as “business as usual.” We assume it’s normal because we grew up with it. Said differently, the world being laid to waste and humans killing each other off long predates AI.

A picturesque mountain village features stone houses with grass-covered roofs and solar panels, set alongside a clear stream crossed by a small wooden bridge. Numerous people are actively tending to vibrant vegetable gardens and harvesting crops throughout the lush, sunlit landscape.

What might a new modernity that's more in alignment with the biosphere look like?
Some thinkers I respect (like Tom Murphy and Vanessa Andreotti, who are both repeat guests on the podcast) say that the way through all this is by abandoning modernity, or hospicing it. I largely agree with their logic, but I wouldn’t call it that. To me, it’s more like redefining humanity. Is a way of life “less modern” if it incorporates a more sophisticated understanding of complexity, ecosystems, and kin, just because it doesn’t have jet airliners doing 100,000 flights a day? I don’t think so. So I’d rather be a champion for a “new” modernity for humans.

What Gives Life Meaning

So that is the work: support bending and not breaking, avoiding nuclear war, midwifing the biosphere, and rekindling the sacred.

The last thing I want to share is one of the burdens of doing this work. There’s a lot to do, but no one knows the future. The timing of what is coming decides who this message is for. I can make a case for the fiscal depression to start in the next five months, in ten years, or sometime later than that. That answer changes who this message is for. If the Great Simplification starts in earnest soon, the response levers are held by the people in power today. If it’s a decade or more away, the most important audience on the planet is currently fifteen- to twenty-year-olds. Because I can’t know, right now our work has to speak to politicians and philanthropists, and to the general public, and to young humans – all at the same time.

How to close this admittedly stark overview of the work? We are not alive at a time of our choosing. The stakes for humanity and the biosphere have never been higher. Ask yourself whether your goals are worthy of the one life you get, because most people only find out they fell short when it’s too late to choose new ones. As I get older, I increasingly feel this.

There is a lot of work to do, and a lot more content coming. Stay tuned, thanks for reading today.

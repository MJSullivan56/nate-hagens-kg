The Great Simplification

_Frankly #34: Limits to Podcasting About Limits_

---

**[Opening]**

Greetings. Just back from a trip to North Carolina, and I almost feel like doing a Frankly about how there was no air conditioning on the airplane, and how the American response to the inconvenience is kind of a microcosm of the human predicament. We were flying across the sky at 700 miles an hour, and it was an affront to the airline industry and to people's lives that there was no air conditioning. But I digress.

On the plane, I wrote some notes. This podcast is a year and a half old, almost, and the theme is The Great Simplification -- which is that our culture, our species, has now consumed beyond our means for 50 years, arguably for hundreds of years. We have kicked many cans, and in the not-too-distant future we're going to have energy, material, financial, social, and ecological limits.

Podcasting about this has limits itself, and I'm unsure of the way forward on this, other than: I'm trying to be a spokesperson for reality, and to hand the mic to other people who are trying to describe our biophysical situation, in order to influence the future in better ways. Here's just a non-inclusive list of things I thought of that are problems or challenges with such a podcast endeavor.

**Defining the audience**

First of all, there are multiple audiences simultaneously. There are people who have followed me for 15 years, who understand overshoot, that money is created with no reference to a biophysical tether, that energy underpins our society and is depleting and is non-fungible with other types of energy, and that the geopolitical situation cannot go back to the way it was in January 2022 -- that we've entered the long emergency in many, many ways. Those people are looking for an advanced hot take on what's going on and how to better prepare our communities, our nations, our families.

Others are the walking worried, who are discovering this information, this channel, this kind of "fly up high and look down at the 64,000-foot view" of the situation. So to put out content that speaks to both of those audiences is difficult. My plan there is to have a Reality 101 primer -- six or seven or eight hours of short videos that describe the whole thing.

Of all the audiences in the world that I think are important -- young people, politicians and leaders, Hollywood, community leaders, and the general public -- my audience for this is the choir, and those people who follow the bat signal of The Great Simplification wherever they are in the world, and try to absorb and pass on the synthesis of this situation. So the challenge: who is the audience for this podcast? That's number one.

**Broadening the scope**

Secondly, lots of people in the world care -- rightly -- about a single issue: climate change, ocean acidification, global inequality, debt, poverty, and all kinds of issues. This story affects all of us. It is a wide, deep, and long-lasting story about the upcoming bottlenecks of the 21st century. So when you incorporate evolutionary biology, politics, renewable energy versus fossil carbon, all of these stories, you are bound to slaughter someone's sacred cow. This is not a single-issue podcast -- it's how the issue of energy and ecological limits affects all the other issues. But that runs the risk of offending people who have their identities or their jobs associated with one issue. It's not that I don't care about those issues; I'm trying to paint the broader story.

**Triple information hazard**

Another challenge with a podcast about this: there is a triple information hazard that exists. Let me explain.

First of all, I know things that I just can't say in a public podcast, because the knowing of those things would make the risk I'm talking about self-fulfilling. So there are certain things that would be interesting and helpful to share, but I just can't do that publicly. That's number one.

Number two: what we're talking about is a shift from the global social-political order of the past. Anything that implies the end of the US empire will occur -- with either a whimper or a bang, but that it might occur -- was one thing when the global tide was rising and lifting all boats; as the global tide is flattening and receding, it becomes threatening to those people and institutions in power. So to speak truth to power eventually becomes threatening, and there's an information hazard in articulating the situation too close to the mark.

Third information hazard, which I wasn't aware of when I started the podcast: I've met amazing people doing Herculean, Superman-type work in the world that I would love to highlight -- what they're doing on conservation and social projects in Africa, alternative finance models. What they're doing is massively inspiring. I want to share it and highlight it to other people, but I can't, because they don't want what they're doing to be made public -- the public awareness of it would short-circuit its own success. So that's a challenge too. I can understand the information hazard about not saying too many bad things, but I also can't say some of the good things, because of the self-limiting ceiling that public awareness would bring.

**Balancing diverse perspectives**

Another challenge, and I'm sure many of you are aware of this: I'm trying to pass the mic to multiple different people from around the world, of different genders, political backgrounds, locations, expertise, etc. -- except on the core issues of biophysical limits that apply to The Great Simplification. To have such diversity runs the risk of diluting the core message that I'm trying to pass the baton on. So it's difficult to have full diversity of voices, which I think is needed in our society, on this podcast. I'm trying to do better about that, and I will try to do better. But I'm going to err on the side of things that are honest and helpful to society, rather than having a fully diversely represented audience. But I'm trying.

Another challenge with this podcast is something I intimated a few Franklys ago, on perception and probability and reality.

**Navigating potential futures**

What do we do? A lot of people are tired of hearing about the problems; they want to hear about solutions. Except as soon as we go into a directed solution, it closes off a lot of other probabilities.

I think there's a chance of a Mad Max outcome -- 20 or so percent, actually higher than 20, because I think there's a 20 percent chance of a nuclear war in the next 18 months, which would obviously precipitate something like that. But there's also a very high percent chance that we continue to kick the can and have a Mordor-economy sort of scenario -- growing our gross energy while the benefits to humans decline, and we still take bigger and bigger bites out of natural ecosystems. That's a high percentage. The Great Simplification -- a financial recalibration, a Great Depression sort of scenario, but things bend and don't break -- is also the midpoint of my distribution.

On the "what to do's," it depends on a global perspective -- whether you're in the Global North or the African subcontinent, whether you're in an area subject to wet-bulb temperatures, whether you're in the United States, or whether you're an environmentalist. The "what to do" depends on many, many things, which is why I'm trying to cast a wide net on getting people's perspectives on what to do.

If I had to simplify it, there are three things that apply in all situations. One is: consume less, especially in the Global North -- that's advice I'd give to almost all listeners. Number two: care about and save the local environment where you are, because at the end of the day, the local ecosystems, the flora, the fauna, the web of life where you live is a treasure that we will miss when it's gone -- and I don't say that often enough, but it's the most important thing. I think we need people becoming aware, waking up, and acting as champions for other species and ecosystems. The third thing is to live your life more holistically -- with yourself and with others -- merging the spiritual, intellectual, emotional, and physical aspects of your life to live as more of a holistic human. This podcast will never be a self-help, meditation, fitness guide, because I'm on these journeys myself. But I do think living more holistically, more openly, aware of the we versus the me, the wider picture, avoiding the fear and dread of the cortisol response and the dopamine "want to get ahead" response -- and there's some oxytocin/serotonin response in the middle of those -- that is the path. But there is not one scenario where you can say "do this because of that." So that's another challenge with this podcast.

**The Stockdale Paradox**

Another thing: there seems to be this sense that -- "here's a big problem, but here's the hopeful thing" -- a kind of toxic positivity that has imbued itself into our culture, where a lot of people think what I talk about is too depressing and too negative, and that's a turn-off.

I think of James Stockdale, and the Stockdale Paradox. He was a prisoner of war, and he noticed that the people who were super pessimistic didn't motivate, got depressed, and didn't do things -- they didn't survive in prison. But the people who were overly optimistic -- "we're going to escape next week, we're going to be released next week" -- they also didn't make it, because they had too many disappointments. It was the realists who ultimately survived. That's what I'm trying to do with this podcast: describe the pathway we're on as best I can, and point the direction into avenues for us as individuals and as a society. So I don't feel like being a rah-rah cheerleader.

What's interesting is that people who think we have no way out of our current situation naturally gravitate to this podcast, because I'm saying a lot of things that are painting a lot of doors shut with what culture says is possible. But I am not that pessimistic -- I'm pessimistic relative to what I used to believe, but I'm optimistic that the future will be better than many of us fear. So there's this challenge of how to be realistic and plausible, but not naively or criminally hopeful, which I don't think is helpful.

**Growth and intent**

Another challenge with podcasting about limits and eventual overshoot and decline: to some people, what I say now seems sensational relative to CNN or other news outlets. In reality, it's not sensational at all, and I have opted not to have sensational titles, sensational graphics, or sensational prose or verbiage on this site, and I never will.

Sensationalism -- and there are many podcasters out there with orders of magnitude more viewers than this one -- sells. People want to be shocked and scared and surprised. I'm just trying to paint, day by day, what I see unfolding. And so what's unfolding over time means my content will almost by definition become less sensational as the world catches up to and surpasses the things I'm talking about. So I don't ever plan on trying to get a lot of views by saying something outrageous to get attention. And that's okay, because this podcast is growing -- it's got about 40,000 subscribers now across the various platforms and on YouTube. I think that's great, and it's great because I know there are other people around the world seeing things similar to me, and it gives me hope that there is some groundswell of rationality, empathy, and collective concern about the future based on reality. But I don't want to grow this 100x, because that's not the point. I want scout teams and catalysts around the world to put this story together and make an impact where they are, and that creates some emergent possibilities for all of us.

**Future direction and outlook**

Lastly -- well, not lastly, but lastly on my mind at the moment -- is the choreography of the future. This is an earth-science-based podcast, at least it started that way. If I wasn't worried about existential risks -- particularly the nuclear war situation between Russia, and the US, and the UK, and also the financial overhang and the economic can we've kicked, with the biophysical Wile E. Coyote moment hanging over us at any point, I would talk about other species. I would have experts on the mass extinction, on ecology, on oceans, on climate, on forests, on soil, all the time, because I think we need an ecologically aware and motivated population of humans.

Having said that -- although that is what I care most about -- I think it's probably going to be the least relevant to our decisions in coming years, and that's going to be economics, poverty, probably wars, and things like that. So, over time, the guests and content on here are going to have to be, unfortunately for what I care about most, more relevant to people's lives than to what I find most valuable and important. But I will not relinquish the core tether to the natural world on the guests and content here. This is what I'm supposed to be doing, I think.

I'm working on many other projects as well, but this podcast is going well. I also wear the mantle of the neo-Malthusians who came before -- that, for various reasons, were not right. Paul Ehrlich didn't know about debt and globalization and some of the technologies. Thomas Malthus didn't know about fossil hydrocarbons and mining the Earth vertically in addition to horizontally.

So, I really don't think this will ever be super popular, because the narrative is too complex, too scary, too departed from what's on the news. But I still value the listeners, the feedback, the emails, the growing conversation -- the tapestry of different voices on how all this stuff fits together. So I hope that little rant was helpful, or at least insightful, into how I'm thinking about all this.

I have some real good Frankly ideas that I need to think about and record, and I will see you next week, hopefully.

Thank you.
